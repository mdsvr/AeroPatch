# Aim the scanners before adding models

AeroPatch finds more real bugs mainly through free deterministic sources aimed at the bug classes it missed. A second small model family adds little. On 2026-10-24 Opengrep and Bandit reported 85 findings across the ten real-CVE repositories, and none landed within 3 lines of the bug ([NOTES.md](../NOTES.md)). That is the expected score, not a broken setup. Only 3 of the 10 CVEs fall inside any published Python SAST benchmark's CWE set. The best free tools recall 28-48% of even those classes. The other seven are logic or resource bugs with no source-to-sink flow to follow. The cheapest gains, in order, come from five sources. First, dependency advisories (pip-audit or OSV-Scanner), shown as a deterministic card because the gates forbid model edits to `requirements*.txt`. Second, a dedicated ReDoS checker. Third, Opengrep's `--taint-intrafile` flag, already shipped in the pinned 1.30.0, with a handful of taint-mode rules. Fourth, Qwen3.5-4B as a judge of pre-selected findings, never as a scanner. Fifth, on the repair side, duplicate-edit rejection and abstract per-CWE fix recipes. Later comes a generated attack test, which is the only known way to let a user's run select among samples the way the oracle-only pass@5 of 67% assumes. How often a 4B model can build one is unmeasured. Adding DeepSeek, Mistral, NVIDIA Nemotron or Google Gemma models is worth one controlled experiment, not a default. Only Nemotron 3 Nano 4B and Ministral 3 3B probably fit 4 GB fully on the GPU. Gemma 4 E2B and E4B fit is unknown until measured. No current DeepSeek model fits with thinking off. No candidate beats Qwen3.5-4B on any published coding number. No study found measures mixes of models under 10B on code repair. Every switch on this laptop also means evicting one model and reloading another. The plan below runs in five phases that slot into doc 17. Each closes on a paired measurement with fixed margins, and the ten real CVEs serve only as a sanity check, because rules written after seeing them would be tuned to them.

## Zero of ten was the expected score for pattern rules

The ten Tier B CVEs cover path traversal in mako, XSS in mistune, code injection in sqlparse, header injection in microdot, a ReDoS, an authentication bypass, two PyJWT signature-verification bugs and two denial-of-service bugs ([evaluations/scenarios](../evaluations/scenarios/)). The only public real-CVE benchmark for Python SAST, from an ICSE 2026 study, covers six CWEs: 77, 502, 94, 79, 22 and 89 ([ICSE'26 study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3744916.3773110); [PyFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07026v1)). Only mako (CWE-22), mistune (CWE-79) and sqlparse (CWE-94) sit inside it. Even there the numbers are low. Over 108 real CVEs, **Semgrep recalls 40.7% at 65.7% precision, CodeQL 31.8% at 72.3%, Bandit 27.8% at 60.0% and Pysa 0%**. The open research prototype PyFlow reaches 48.1% recall at 71.2% precision ([PyFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07026v1)). The two classes AeroPatch's in-scope CVEs belong to are the weakest. About 70% of the XSS cases went undetected by every tool combined, and Bandit missed 26 of 29 path traversals ([ICSE'26 study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3744916.3773110)).

The 60% Bandit precision does not contradict the 11.34% quoted in the earlier non-technical-users report ([SAGA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.15154)). The two studies use different datasets and count false positives differently. The ICSE study counts a report on the patched version as a false positive. What matters for AeroPatch is that both put Bandit near the bottom on precision. The ICSE study also found that 25 of Bandit's 30 detections were found by CodeQL or Semgrep too ([ICSE'26 study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3744916.3773110)).

AeroPatch's own rules explain the rest. All 22 rules in [aeropatch-python.yml](../rules/aeropatch-python.yml) are pattern rules, and none uses `mode: taint`. They were written for the hand-built Tier A scenarios. One of them, `redos-nested-quantifier`, exists and still missed the real ReDoS. Pattern rules catch "misuse of specific, well-known functions" such as `eval()` or `pickle.load()`, which is where Semgrep scored best ([ICSE'26 study](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3744916.3773110)). A JWT library that accepts the wrong algorithm, or an HPACK decoder without a size cap, contains no such call. A realistic ceiling for taint analysis on these ten is therefore **about 1-3 of 10, plus a dedicated checker for the ReDoS one**.

The 0 of 10 also measures a narrower task than the one users face. Each Tier B repository *is* the vulnerable library, so the benchmark asks AeroPatch to find a bug inside library source. A user's app that pins a vulnerable mako or PyJWT is a different case. An advisory scanner would flag that by name and version, which is probably close to 10 of 10 when the vulnerable versions are pinned. That figure is an inference. pip-audit cannot measure it on Tier B, because a library's own repository does not depend on itself. The report should therefore score detection in two layers: library-source detection (today 0 of 10) and downstream detection through advisories.

The user-facing evidence points the same way. On the author's own GitHub repositories, in an informal trial, most findings were false alarms. The one local fix tried looked plausible and was harmful. Phase 0 measured that "Scanner quiet, not tested" is right **23 of 43** times and "Scanner quiet, your tests still pass" **25 of 34** ([NOTES.md](../NOTES.md)). So the bottleneck on real code sits before the model: what gets flagged, and in what order.

## A precision-first funnel puts free deterministic sources ahead of any model

Dependency advisories are the highest-precision source and cost no GPU. A finding names an exact CVE and a fixed version. Their known noise comes from two places. One is resolution: OSV-Scanner's deps.dev-based transitive resolution for `requirements.txt` does not reproduce pip's interpreter, platform or constraints ([OneUptime](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-07-23-requirements-false-positives-osv/view)). The other is reachability: the vulnerable function may never be called. A grep for the advisory package's import covers the second cheaply.

Three repository facts shape how advisories fit in. First, `requirements*.txt`, `pyproject.toml` and `*.lock` are in `FORBIDDEN_GLOBS` ([config.py](../src/aeropatch/config.py)), so the model's patch loop cannot bump a pin. The advisory belongs on a deterministic card like the secrets card: package, installed version, fixed version, and the one-line edit for the user to make. Second, neither pip-audit nor OSV-Scanner is installed today. Bandit is the only scanner dependency ([pyproject.toml](../pyproject.toml)). Third, both look up an online advisory database, so package names and versions leave the laptop. The privacy line "your code stays on this laptop" still holds. The advisory lookup should still be a named step the user can see, and its offline caching was not verified.

ReDoS is the only missed class with mature free checkers. A systematisation of ReDoS tools reports regexploit with low false-positive and false-negative rates, and safe-regex and redos-detector with much higher false-positive rates ([SoK, arXiv 2406.11618](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.11618)). Parolini et al.'s analyser terminates in under a second on 99.47% of 74,670 regexes ([TASE 2022](https://www-apr.lip6.fr/~mine/publi/article-parolini-al-tase22.pdf)). Running regexploit over the repository's regex literals is CPU-only and needs no model. Its licence was not checked.

Taint analysis is already on disk. On this laptop, `opengrep scan --help` for 1.30.0 lists `--taint-intrafile`: "Enable intra-file inter-procedural taint analysis", with Python among the supported languages. Semgrep moved cross-function taint to its commercial platform in late 2024, and the Opengrep fork restores it within a single file ([appsecsanta](https://appsecsanta.com/opengrep-vs-semgrep); [Semgrep feature definitions](https://docs.semgrep.dev/references/feature-definitions)). Because every AeroPatch rule is a pattern rule, the flag does nothing until some rules are rewritten in taint mode. The natural first set has three rules. The first covers request data reaching a response-header setter without a CR/LF check (microdot's class). The second covers request data reaching `open` or `send_file`. The third covers request data reaching `eval` or `exec`. Two pattern rules join them. One flags `jwt.decode` with `verify_signature: False`, without `algorithms=`, with the algorithm taken from the token header, or with `algorithms=jwt.algorithms.get_default_algorithms()`. The last is the app-side exposure for PyJWT's CVE-2022-29217 ([GitLab advisory](https://advisories.gitlab.com/pkg/pypi/pyjwt/CVE-2022-29217/)). The other flags unbounded reads and decompression of request bodies. None of these finds the bug inside PyJWT or hpack. Advisories cover those.

One coupling must not be missed. `run_opengrep` is also the re-scan behind `original_rule_present`, which decides the "Scanner quiet" labels ([scanners.py](../src/aeropatch/tools/scanners.py)). The 25 of 34 figure was measured through that exact code. Any new flag or rule therefore needs a new config name, as the README's rule requires. It also needs the oracle replay rerun, about 15 minutes with no model, before 25 of 34 is quoted again.

Licences rule out the two strongest-looking shortcuts. CodeQL's terms permit analysis only of codebases "released under an OSI-approved License". They forbid use "in connection with any codebase that is not an Open Source Codebase (e.g., code in a private repo in GitHub)" without a paid Advanced Security licence ([codeql-cli-binaries LICENSE.md](https://github.com/github/codeql-cli-binaries/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). So CodeQL, and IRIS built on it, can at most be an opt-in for public open-source repositories. Semgrep's registry rules carry the Semgrep Rules License v1.0: "You may use the rules only for your own internal business purposes. This license does not allow you to distribute the rules" ([Semgrep Rules License](https://semgrep.dev/legal/rules-license/)). AeroPatch cannot bundle them. The community opengrep-rules repository is reported read-only, and its licence text was not read ([opengrep-rules](https://github.com/opengrep/opengrep-rules/blob/main/LICENSE)). Doc 17 deferred "a larger community Opengrep ruleset" until "Phase 0's scan finds few of the 10 real advisories" ([17-easy-use-part1.md](../docs/17-easy-use-part1.md)). Phase 0 found none, so the trigger has fired. The answer is still no bulk import. The registry is unbundleable, the open repository's licence is unverified, and more pattern rules do not reach seven of the ten missed classes. Pysa should be dropped: 0% recall on the real benchmark ([PyFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07026v1)).

A small model helps detection only when a static tool has already pointed it somewhere. Two results set the limits. In IRIS, an LLM proposes taint specifications for CodeQL to run. That doubled detections on Java from 27 to 55 of 120 with GPT-4. Small models kept most of the recall gain: DeepSeekCoder 7B found 52 and Llama 3 8B found 41. They lost precision, at a **false discovery rate of about 95%** against 85% for GPT-4 ([IRIS](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.17238v3)). Scanning function by function, on the other hand, is close to random. A 7B model fell from 68.26% F1 on BigVul to 3.09% on the cleaner PrimeVul ([PrimeVul](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18624)).

Triage of tool output is where models earn their keep. One system pairing Semgrep with a fine-tuned LLM reached 89.5% precision, against 35.7% for Semgrep alone ([SAST-Genius](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.15433v3)). That result was seen only in a search snippet, and its backbone size was not read. An agentic triage study warns that aggressive filtering suppresses real bugs ([Sifting the Noise](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.22952.md)).

Vulnhuntr, the one Python-specific LLM hunter, says plainly that open models served through Ollama fail its structured output ([protectai/vulnhuntr](https://github.com/protectai/vulnhuntr)). Its reusable idea is the call-chain context fetch, not the tool. The role that fits Qwen3.5-4B is a JSON-constrained "keep or park, with a one-line reason" verdict on each finding the funnel already produced. It should lean toward keep and never delete. At 46 tok/s, a 200-token verdict is roughly 5 seconds per finding, by estimate, so a few dozen per check is affordable.

Authentication bypass is the one class nothing deterministic reaches. It stays an opt-in "worth a look" review of auth decorators, and comes later.

## Repair gains come from selection and knowledge, not a second family

With the exploit test hidden, the realistic resolve rates are **46% on the 27 test scenarios and 40% on the 10 CVEs** (25 of 54 and 8 of 20 runs). These, not 70% and 35%, are the rates a user's folder can expect ([NOTES.md](../NOTES.md)). The pass@5 of 67% and 50% looks like headroom. But pass@5 counts a task as solved when any sample passes the hidden suites, which is oracle selection ([README](../README.md)). A user's run has to pick a sample without those suites.

The evidence on runtime selection is sobering. In Agentless (GPT-4o, SWE-bench Lite), majority voting alone solved 25.67%, and regression-test filtering lifted it to 27.00%. Reproduction tests added the next 5 points, to 32.00% ([Agentless v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01489v2)). For AeroPatch, sampling without a generated attack test is therefore worth about a point. With one, it may be worth several, but only on the findings where a test can be built. The attack test is already doc 17's Phase 3 witness template ([17-easy-use-part5.md](../docs/17-easy-use-part5.md)), so best-of-n belongs after it, not before.

The witness build rate is the open number. Frontier agents build working proofs of vulnerability for only **16-51%** of real bugs, and the top figure had the advisory and the fix as input ([FaultLine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15241); [CVE-Genie](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01835v1)). In one agentic testing study, about 44% of the agent's "this test exposes a bug" claims were wrong ([arXiv 2510.09907](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.09907)). Nobody has measured how often a 4B model filling a per-CWE template succeeds. Phase 3 measures it, and "no witness could be built" should be planned as the common case.

Phase 0 also found the opposite failure. In 9 of V1's 20 "not fixed" tasks, some attempt passed both hidden suites, but the rule still fired or a test failed, so nothing was offered ([NOTES.md](../NOTES.md)). Some of the lost ground is in the selector's strictness, not the model's skill.

Three cheap repair changes have support. The first is duplicate-edit rejection: normalise the SEARCH/REPLACE pair, refuse an edit already tried, and resample. Repeated edits after feedback are documented for base instruct models ([RLEF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02089)), and the fine-tuned AeroPatch runs failed partly as `STUCK` ([README](../README.md)). It costs one extra generation, about 7 seconds by estimate.

The second is abstract per-CWE fix recipes for the classes the local model misses. One reads "derive with a per-user random salt and a KDF, compare with `hmac.compare_digest`". Another reads "resolve the host, reject private and link-local IPs, connect to the checked IP". A third reads "resolve the member path and require it to stay under the destination". In VulKey, abstract patterns of CWE, edit action and key element added 7.6 points of exact match. A bare CWE ID added nothing, and concrete retrieved examples fell below baseline ([VulKey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01769)). That study used C/C++ and fine-tuned 15-32B models, so the effect has to be re-measured at 4B.

The third is the attack test, discussed above. Keeping edits small is a constraint rather than a fourth technique, and the gates already enforce it. In Vul4Py, 12.6% of exploit-passing patches broke functional tests, mostly from agents making larger edits ([Vul4Py](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.00692)). In PatchEval, success fell from 32.5% at 1-5 changed lines to 2.1% at over 50 ([PatchEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11019v1)).

Two familiar levers stay off. Thinking mode scored 41 against 38 of 54, under the project's bar of 4 ([README](../README.md)). In the literature, thinking variants matched or trailed their non-thinking twins on vulnerability repair, and Qwen3-8B fixed 0 of 23 Python CVEs either way ([PatchEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11019v1); [VulnRepairEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.03331v1)). Fine-tuning tied or lost ([NOTES.md](../NOTES.md)). GRPO on a free T4 means about 80 seconds a step against the 300 or more steps Unsloth says are needed ([Unsloth RL guide](https://unsloth.ai/docs/get-started/reinforcement-learning-rl-guide)).

The evidence for every model under 10B is thin. None of these candidates has a published Aider, SWE-bench, HumanEval+ or security-repair score. The one shared coding benchmark is vendor-reported, mostly in thinking mode, and AeroPatch runs with thinking off. On it, every candidate that could fit trails Qwen by 1 to 12 points. The gaps of 1 to 4 points to Ministral, Gemma 4 E4B and Nemotron sit within the noise of mixed versions and modes.

Gemma 4's fit hinges on how Ollama places its per-layer embedding tables. The technical report gives quantised core weights of 0.8 GB for E2B and 2.3 GB for E4B, yet the Ollama files are 4.6 and 6.6 GB ([Gemma 4 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.02770)).

The thinking switch differs by family, and each needs checking. Nemotron thinks by default and is turned off by system prompt or `enable_thinking=False` ([Nemotron card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-4B-BF16)). Gemma 4 thinks only when the system prompt starts with `<|think|>`. The DeepSeek distills cannot stop thinking at all, which alone disqualifies them as patch writers here. Whether Ollama 0.40.10's `think: false` actually reaches Nemotron's switch was not verified. Both the Gemma 4 and Nemotron tags were re-uploaded in the last one to three weeks, so a benchmark must pin the digest.

Even a model that fits faces a high bar as a companion. Mixing raises the oracle union far more than a selector can harvest. On SWE-bench Verified, five strong systems together covered **80.8%**, but CodeMonkeys' selector picked 66.2%, against 62.8% for the best member and 60.9% for random choice ([CodeMonkeys](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14723)). Sampling one model several times beat mixing models by 6.6 points on AlpacaEval 2.0, because "mixing different LLMs often lowers the average quality" ([Self-MoA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00674)). And when two models are both wrong, they pick the same wrong answer about 60% of the time ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07962v1)). A second family therefore pays only if its pass@1 is close to Qwen's and it solves tasks no Qwen sample solves.

The roles rank by evidence. First comes a voter on binary triage, where majority voting gave its largest gain, 3.1 points over the best single model on vulnerability detection ([Beyond ChatGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.01001v1)). Second is a patch writer, but only one whose pass@1 is near Qwen's. Third is a test writer from a different family. No study measures that role, but a second family's tests should not share the patch author's blind spots. A judge comes last. Small judges swing up to 14 points when the two candidates swap order ([CodeJudgeBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10535)), and they favour their own family's outputs ([arXiv 2604.06996](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.06996)).

Switching has a concrete price on this laptop. Qwen takes 3,043 MiB at 8k, so no second 2.8-3 GB model can sit beside it. Ollama loads a new GPU model only if it fits fully in VRAM, and otherwise unloads the idle one ([Ollama FAQ](https://docs.ollama.com/faq)).

A reload costs the disk read plus a few seconds of CUDA setup. The disk read is about 1-3 seconds from NVMe, 15-25 seconds from a hard disk, and near zero while Windows still holds the file in its cache. These are estimates, not measurements, and should be checked with the `load_duration` field Ollama returns.

In the benchmark, batching makes switching negligible: all Qwen samples for all tasks, unload with `keep_alive: 0`, then all of model B. Interleaving per task would cost up to about 270 switches on 27 tasks with five samples per model. In the product, a second role costs at least two switches per check run, and only if every finding is batched through each model. It also costs the user a second roughly 3 GB download and a second digest for the doctor to check. Keeping `OLLAMA_MAX_LOADED_MODELS=1` and `OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL=1` is a cheap guard. Neither is shown to prevent the commit-memory failure (WinError 1455) seen earlier beside Docker.

Disk is the other practical limit. The four new candidate files total about 16 GB, against 7.7 GB free on F:. The bake-off must either pull, measure and `ollama rm` one model at a time, or move the model store to D:, which has about 298 GB free. Moving it is the owner's call. Load times must then be measured from wherever the running server actually reads, and D: must be mounted. The 5.6 GB of v3 fine-tunes on F: ([NOTES.md](../NOTES.md)) are also the owner's to keep or delete.

## Five phases, each closed by a paired measurement

The statistics set what each phase can show. On 27 tasks, one pass rate has a standard error of about 9-10 points. On 10 tasks it is about 15 points (computed from the binomial). Every comparison below is therefore paired per task (or per finding), and every arm gets the same budget of generations. The protocol pre-registers the arms, runs each three times with alternated seeds, and tests with an exact McNemar test on the tasks where the arms disagree ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00640)). A union of k samples from A and k from B is compared with 2k samples of A alone, never with k.

The unit of the test is the task, not the task-run. Each arm's outcome on a task is the majority of its three runs (solved in at least 2 of 3). McNemar then counts the tasks where the two arms' majorities differ. Pooling 81 task-runs would treat repeats of one task as independent evidence, which they are not. The project's existing bar, a difference of 4 or more of 54 over two runs ([README](../README.md)), stays as the first screen. A gain claimed in this plan must clear both that bar and the McNemar margin.

| Tasks where exactly one arm succeeds | 6 | 9 | 12 | 15 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Split needed for p < 0.05 (two-sided exact McNemar) | 6-0 | 8-1 | 10-2 | 12-3 | 14-4 |

The ten real CVEs answer only one question: does the change ever solve or find one that the baseline never does? Detection uses the same thresholds on a held-out set chosen before any rule is written. Every proposed bar below follows doc 17's convention: the owner can change it before the runs, not after.

The deployable layer uses one pre-registered selector for both arms: walk the samples in a fixed order (alternating Qwen and B in the mix arm), offer the first that earns "Scanner quiet, your tests still pass", and count the task as solved if that offered sample passes the hidden suites. Phase A comes first because every later detection claim needs a set that was frozen before the rules existed. Without it, the new taint rules would be scored on the ten CVEs they were written to catch.

Phase B is mostly configuration and short YAML. The advisory card and the ReDoS checker are new dependencies, so their licences are checked first, as doc 17 did for codemodder.

Phase C is the only phase where a model enters detection, and it enters as a filter on paths a tool produced. That is the shape that survived at 7-8B in IRIS.

Phase D keeps the single model and changes only what the evidence favours. Its first step reads existing run files and costs minutes. The other two are prompt or harness changes, each measured as a new config.

Phase E answers the user's model question with one pre-registered comparison rather than four exploratory ones. Trying several candidates on the test split and reporting the best would inflate false positives. Its cost is an estimate drawn from Phase 0's measured 11 minutes per three-attempt test run ([17-easy-use-part2.md](../docs/17-easy-use-part2.md)). A one-sample pass over 27 tasks probably takes 4-6 minutes, so 15 passes per seed is roughly 1.5 hours and three seeds about 4-5 hours. That is an attended job, started resumably with `--run-id`, after checking Ollama's `app.log` for a staged update, with nothing heavy running beside it.

## Conclusion

The question "which models should AeroPatch add?" turns out to be downstream of a question it had not measured until 2026-10-24: what reaches the model at all. Detection, triage and selection all sit before or after generation. Every one of them can be improved with free, CPU-only tools or a single Qwen call, and measured without a second model. The surprise in the evidence is how little the classic "ensemble of diverse models" story survives at this scale. Unions grow, selectors do not keep up, errors correlate, and the one sub-10B result found is single-family sampling with execution checks. On a 4 GB laptop, a second family is less a quality lever than a cost: a download, a digest, a reload, and a doubled evaluation budget.

What changes is how AeroPatch should describe itself. The honest headline is not "finds and fixes real CVEs". It is "flags known-vulnerable dependencies precisely, finds a few classes of flaw in your own code, and proposes fixes it labels by what was checked". The 0 of 10 then becomes a published library-source number beside a downstream advisory number, instead of a hidden embarrassment. The model-mix question gets one clean, pre-registered answer instead of a slow drift into four models that no user can afford to download.
